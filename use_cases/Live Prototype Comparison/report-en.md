## Summary

Prioritize space for close reading and decision checks on individual results
while retaining queue-state visibility. A is preferred more often, and B's
status grouping also has clear value. Both help people understand limited
authorization, but process advancement and status confirmation cannot replace
content meeting requirements.

- Of 20, 14 prefer A, 4 prefer B and 2 have no meaningful preference. The main
  tradeoff is close-reading space versus continuous queue awareness.
- Feedback history and diff marks support checking, but some still refuse
  authorization or withhold acceptance because content/evidence is insufficient.
- Acceptance, authorization and actual completion are fairly distinct. Message-body
  placement, the decline-reason entry and receipt terminology retain local friction.
- Synthetic Persona feedback, not research with real participants or a population estimate.

## Findings

<a id="finding-f1"></a>

### Prioritize close-reading space without sacrificing queue visibility

Fourteen of 20 choose A, chiefly for a wider body area and adjacent task list or
decision controls. Participants want to check drafts, revisions, destinations
and agreed content carefully. B's narrow reading area after opening details,
scrolling and truncated titles create a space tradeoff.

The 4 choosing B prioritize continuous awareness of pending, in-progress and
handled work. They tolerate some scrolling even while acknowledging limited
reading space. Improvement should not simply remove the board. The 2 without
meaningful preference reserve judgment on assistive-technology usability and a
complete review comparison respectively; this does not establish equivalence.

Evidence contributors: 20 from cited supporting answers; 1 from cited counter answers. These sets may overlap and are not votes on every claim.

<details><summary>View evidence details</summary>

- Respondents checking documents, changes or decision support generally say Design A keeps task/decision information visible while providing more reading space. Design B's details narrow the reading area, add scrolling or truncate titles; some explicitly value reading room over continuous board visibility. (supporting answers: 18; counter answers: 1)
- Fourteen choose Design A for the wider content area and adjacent list/decision controls. They want careful checks of drafts, revisions, destinations and agreed content; some explicitly accept scrolling before reading rather than compress reading to retain the whole board. (supporting answers: 14; counter answers: 6)
- Four choose Design B for distinguishing work needing input, continuing work and handled items, with the board visible during review. They intend short, intermittent follow-up, still acknowledging limited reading space and sometimes tolerating additional scrolling. (supporting answers: 4; counter answers: 14)
- Two select neither: one prioritizes unverified keyboard/screen-reader usability over layout; the other only saved a draft in B and cannot compare a complete planning review. Both recognize local strengths without sufficient basis for an overall choice. (supporting answers: 2; counter answers: 5)
- To validate: Paths differ and order is fixed. Preferences/spatial experience do not establish efficiency or error-rate differences.

</details>

### Design B - Status columns help locate intervention points

All 20 answers express understanding of matters awaiting them or of status
sections. Participants seek work needing attention; headings distinguish stages,
and some use card movement to understand that a decision/request was recorded.

Queue value coexists with reading burden. With details open, narrower board space
and truncated titles hinder some scanning, although one explicitly says this
did not affect reading the draft. Not every task is hard to read, and movement
is not real work completion.

Evidence contributors: 20 from cited supporting answers; 0 from cited counter answers. These sets may overlap and are not votes on every claim.

<details><summary>View evidence details</summary>

- All 20 understand matters awaiting them or status sections. They see Design B as checking Agent progress and identifying work needing intervention, not simply a chat entry. (supporting answers: 20; counter answers: 0)
- Participants seek work needing attention/decisions. Headings distinguish pending, in-progress and handled tasks; some specifically say movement confirms recording a request/decision. (supporting answers: 19; counter answers: 0)
- Several report a narrower board/truncated titles after opening details; some also find document/message reading areas narrow, shallow or frequently wrapped. One explicitly says the narrower board did not affect draft reading, so the problem is not universal. (supporting answers: 18; counter answers: 1)
- To validate: No controlled multi-item prioritization accuracy or reading-time evidence was supplied. Understanding B may include learning from A.

<a id="finding-f3"></a>

### Design A - Revision cues support checking, not automatic acceptance

Participants want to know whether changes meet goals, not merely whether a version
advanced. Nine answers say diff marks locate revisions; 3 say earlier feedback
shown with the revision helps check whether it was addressed.

Three still refuse sending or withhold acceptance for unmet content: 2 request
next week's plan, and 1 cannot find a requested explanation in the sample
revision. Preserve human content judgment rather than equating a new version
or feedback record with successful acceptance.

Evidence contributors: 11 from cited supporting answers; 4 from cited counter answers. These sets may overlap and are not votes on every claim.

<details><summary>View evidence details</summary>

- Nine say strikethrough, addition marks or red/green diffs help locate revisions; several use them to check corrections to “authorization is not completion/successful delivery.” Three also say co-presented earlier feedback helps judge whether revisions address it. (supporting answers: 9; counter answers: 0)
- Document review relies on feedback, diffs and completion criteria; research-scope decisions rely on scope/time cost. Two explicitly identify the missing next-week plan, giving grounds for refusal/withholding distinct from accepting the sample. (supporting answers: 11; counter answers: 0)
- Three report refusing sending or not accepting because content is missing: 2 require next week's plan before rereview, and 1 finds their requested explanation absent. This preserves a path other than straightforward sample acceptance. (supporting answers: 3; counter answers: 4)
- To validate: These judgments concern specific synthetic artifacts. Unimplemented simulated feedback does not establish defects in a real Agent's revision capability.

</details>

<a id="finding-f4"></a>

### Design B - Clear boundaries can coexist with withholding approval

All 20 understand the limited consequences of their actions, without replacing
content judgment. Six report refusing authorization/withholding acceptance for
missing plans, unverifiable claims, unsuitable wording or omitted revisions;
another 6 report accepting v3.

Participants compare requests, change marks and returned text to decide whether
content is sufficient. Acceptance and withholding concern different paths, not
direct disagreement about the same output. A traceable process is not reliable
content.

Evidence contributors: 20 from cited supporting answers; 3 from cited counter answers. These sets may overlap and are not votes on every claim.

<details><summary>View evidence details</summary>

- All 20 understand limited consequences: document acceptance is not publishing; authorization binds version/destination and is not delivery; drafts/notes are local records; requesting revision is not automatic acceptance. Nobody reports needing to undo afterward, although one corrects Agent selection before saving. (supporting answers: 20; counter answers: 0)
- Six refuse authorization/withhold acceptance for missing plans, unverifiable claims, unsuitable wording or omitted revisions. Another 6 accept v3, including one reading changes first. This is path-dependent judgment, not uniform distrust. (supporting answers: 6; counter answers: 6)
- Participants compare earlier feedback, marks and returned text. Clear authorization-versus-delivery explanations support sample acceptance, but unresolved wording, missing examples or completion-check explanations still block it. (supporting answers: 4; counter answers: 3)
- To validate: These are self-reported judgments about synthetic content. Unmet requests do not establish real Agent/product revision defects.

</details>

<a id="finding-f5"></a>

### Limited permission is distinguished from completion

Relevant answers in both designs distinguish local saving, document acceptance,
one-time permission for a specified version/destination and final confirmation.
Participants want to avoid approving the wrong object: acceptance does not
publish, and authorization does not deliver.

Execution summaries record 20 first successful clicks on Authorization, 14 on
Authorize once and 14 on simulated-receipt generation. These verify control
attempts, not thorough review, satisfaction or real external completion. Nobody
in B reporting a need to undo also does not validate undo capability.

Evidence contributors: 20 from cited supporting answers; 0 from cited counter answers. These sets may overlap and are not votes on every claim.

<details><summary>View evidence details</summary>

- Participants understand that creating a draft does not start an Agent and accepting a document does not authorize publishing. Confirmations match expectations. (supporting answers: 13; counter answers: 0)
- Authorization is understood as one-time permission for a specified version/destination, not continuing access or delivery proof. Waiting for and seeing simulated receipts helps confirm current outcome. (supporting answers: 16; counter answers: 0)
- All 20 understand limited consequences: acceptance is not publishing; authorization binds version/destination and is not delivery; drafts/notes are local; revision requests are not automatic acceptance. Nobody reports needing undo afterward; one corrects Agent selection before saving. (supporting answers: 20; counter answers: 0)

</details>

### Design A - The authorization message still requires searching below

A's persistent list and side decision panel help retain location, find next
steps and check authorization scope. Six answers explicitly say the message
body follows authorization details and requires scrolling.

Participants usually accept additional reading for content checks. Not all
scrolling is wasted effort. The specific problem is that the body needed for
a sending decision and authorization information are not both easily accessible.

Evidence contributors: 20 from cited supporting answers; 1 from cited counter answers. These sets may overlap and are not votes on every claim.

<details><summary>View evidence details</summary>

- Persistent list/side decision information supports orientation, next steps and authorization-scope checks; 6 explicitly say the message follows authorization details and requires scrolling. (supporting answers: 17; counter answers: 0)
- Participants generally accept scrolling/tab switching needed to read messages, documents or revisions, intending to inspect content, evidence or records first. Extra actions are not universally unnecessary. (supporting answers: 18; counter answers: 1)
- To validate: No scrolling-time, misoperation or decision-accuracy evidence was supplied.

### Declining and recording a reason are not directly connected

A minority in both designs are unsure where to record revisions/reasons after
declining. In A, one answer separates the reason entry from the decision; in B,
one still has to find Save note.

Participants want to record reasons/follow-up after withholding permission.
This is local continuity between decisions and feedback, not overall navigation
failure; existing note capability must not be described as missing.

Evidence contributors: 10 from cited supporting answers; 8 from cited counter answers. These sets may overlap and are not votes on every claim.

<details><summary>View evidence details</summary>

- Some are uncertain when seeking research findings, requesting changes after refusal, returning to correct accepted content or judging Agent suitability. These concern specific points, not unclear overall purpose. (supporting answers: 4; counter answers: 2)
- Three answers respectively report separated refusal/reason entry, a narrow feedback field making long requests hard to reread, and cross-tab source searches without needed details. They are distinct burdens, not overall navigation failure. (supporting answers: 3; counter answers: 4)
- Despite understanding the board, a minority remain unsure about grounds for accepted status, message-body location, refusal-reason entry or task-scope detail. Another draft answer lacks clarity on adding information and continuing. (supporting answers: 5; counter answers: 4)
- After easy basic actions, some still seek the next step after saving or Save note. Another finds further browsing unhelpful after not locating checklist details. (supporting answers: 3; counter answers: 2)
- To validate: Self-report does not establish that everyone intending follow-up saved reasons, or identify a shared root cause.

</details>

### Receipts still need plain-language explanation

Most relevant answers distinguish authorization from delivery, but a minority
in A are unsure about execution receipts/technical action names. One in B
requests plain-language explanations/examples of execution receipts and where
to check results.

Participants want to know what evidence confirms an outcome. Understanding the
permission boundary and understanding completion evidence are two judgments;
clarity about the former does not cancel local confusion about the latter.

Evidence contributors: 15 from cited supporting answers; 3 from cited counter answers. These sets may overlap and are not votes on every claim.

<details><summary>View evidence details</summary>

- Several say post-authorization notices clearly distinguish recorded permission from unconfirmed delivery. A minority remain unsure about execution-receipt/technical-action terminology. (supporting answers: 8; counter answers: 2)
- Several distinguish acceptance/publishing, authorization/delivery and local drafts/starting an Agent; one still asks for plain-language receipt explanations/examples and where to view outcomes. (supporting answers: 11; counter answers: 1)
- To validate: Receipts are simulated. Terminology uncertainty is not total misunderstanding of authorization or universal failure of existing explanations.

</details>

<a id="finding-f10"></a>

### Natural stopping is not content satisfaction or completed work

Participants generally want to establish where the current item stands, not clear
the entire workspace. Acceptance, refusal, scope choice or simulated confirmation
can all be stopping points. Some stop with unmet content requirements or without
detailed evidence. Stopping cannot substitute for satisfaction or a completed
supervision cycle.

Custom-task paths establish centralized requirements and clear boundaries, not
target output. A minority are unsure how to correct accepted content or locate
specific evidence. Entry-point discoverability needs validation rather than
claims that correction/evidence does not exist.

Evidence contributors: 20 from cited supporting answers; 3 from cited counter answers. These sets may overlap and are not votes on every claim.

<details><summary>View evidence details</summary>

- Six describe stopping after acceptance, a simulated receipt or no remaining attention items. They intend to confirm the selected item's position, not finish the whole workspace. (supporting answers: 6; counter answers: 3)
- Three retain a different ending: refusing sending or viewing returned revisions before stopping, with content needs still unmet. (supporting answers: 3; counter answers: 3)
- Ten report sufficient understanding after acceptance, refusal, scope choice or simulated receipt. This usually ends the current review point, not validation of real-work fit. (supporting answers: 10; counter answers: 1)
- Four stop after understanding tracking/review but with unmet content requirements or no detailed evidence. This preserves “understood process, did not accept result.” (supporting answers: 4; counter answers: 1)
- Custom-task participants say saving requirements prepares work, centralizes requests or states privacy/review boundaries. They did not obtain supplier messages, study materials, schedule updates, catalog updates, budget analyses or handover checklists. (supporting answers: 6; counter answers: 0)
- Five rely on notices that no Agent starts, files are not accessed and drafts stay local. Some also inspect saved details, confirmation or Drafts. Continuation questions concern supplying input, the receiving Agent, output form and factual support. (supporting answers: 5; counter answers: 0)
- Some are uncertain about research findings, revision after refusal, correction of accepted content or Agent suitability. These are specific comprehension points, not unclear overall purpose. (supporting answers: 4; counter answers: 2)
- To validate: Local simulation limits later output. Reasons for not finding evidence and correction paths after acceptance lack complete validation.

</details>

## Limitations

This report concerns self-selected paths from 20 synthetic participants, without
segments. Controlled multi-item prioritization, complete assistive-technology
testing and matched-path comparison evidence are absent. Everyone sees A then B;
learning, anchoring, carryover and recency may apply, precluding causal layout
superiority. Tasks, authorization, revisions and receipts are local simulations.
First successful clicks do not prove thorough review, real execution or
satisfaction. Unvisited paths are not failure evidence.
