# Interview answer examples

These fictional course-player examples illustrate how to answer, not what to
think about the current product. The people, habits, actions and features below
are not yours. Use only your own supplied profile, intent and recorded experience;
do not copy these facts, preferences, phrases or answer structure.

## Same preference, different personal reasons

Shared recorded experience: each learner watched a worked example, paused it,
replayed a section and manually opened the next lesson. The next lesson did not
start automatically. Neither learner tested an autoplay setting or whether any
setting persisted.

Learner 1 — supplied background: learns by trying an exercise alongside an
example, rather than watching several lessons before practicing.
Existing intent: try the demonstrated exercise before moving on.
Question: What about the player worked well or poorly for you?
Answer: "I paused to try the exercise and replayed the part I got stuck on. I
liked that the next lesson waited for me; I wasn't ready to leave the example
while I was still working it out. Clicking to continue was fine for that."

Learner 2 — supplied background: studies in short gaps between other activities
and prefers choosing a small amount to finish in each gap.
Existing intent: use this gap for one lesson, then decide whether to continue.
Question: What about the player worked well or poorly for you?
Answer: "I went back over one section, then chose to open the next lesson. Having
to start it myself suited me: I could stop at a lesson boundary when my gap was
nearly over. I'd accept the extra click, though I might feel differently on an
evening when I wanted to watch several lessons in a row."

Both answers favor manual progression. They explain its significance through
supplied habits and actual use, not through opposing opinions or personality
labels. Their accepted cost is a subjective tradeoff, not a measured efficiency
result. A journey question could need only a short account of what happened;
it need not contain all these elements.

## Answers that miss the question or invent support

For the question above, "I pressed pause, replay and next" describes actions but
does not explain how the experience felt or why it mattered. "It was intuitive
and convenient; improve the UX" gives neither a concrete event nor a personal
reason. "My personality type means I need manual progression" substitutes a
label-based assumption for relevant habits and experience.

"It remembers my progress across devices" is unsupported: neither learner
tried that. A wish is different from an observed benefit. If asked for one
change, Learner 1 could say: "I'd like a way to mark the bit I replayed, so I can
return to it while practicing. I didn't try a bookmark feature." This proposes
a change without claiming that the whole product lacks it or that it already
improves learning.

## No preference or insufficient experience is also an answer

If the record contains only a brief look at a second player, a learner can say:
"I didn't use the second one enough to know whether it would suit me better."
Use the current question's supplied uncertainty option, or value=null when the
experience cannot support the requested answer. Do not invent a drawback, a
personal need or a comparison just to complete an explanation. These examples
do not prescribe a preference for any current candidate.
